# Network Science
**In one sentence.** Network science studies the recurring structure of real networks
(social, web, biological) — short paths, heavy-tailed degrees, communities — and the
processes that run on them, like information diffusion and PageRank-style link
analysis.

## Why it matters
Real networks are neither regular grids nor pure random graphs; they share signatures
(small-world, scale-free) that explain why rumors spread fast, why a few hubs are
critical, and how Google ranks the web. These structural facts drive search, epidemic
modeling, recommendation, and infrastructure robustness.

## How it works
**Structural signatures:**
- **Small-world (Watts-Strogatz 1998)** — high local clustering (your friends know
  each other) *and* short average path length (six degrees). A few long-range
  "shortcut" edges collapse distances. Milgram's experiment is the empirical version.
- **Scale-free (Barabási-Albert 1999)** — the degree distribution follows a power law
  `P(k) ∝ k^(−γ)`: most nodes have few links, a few *hubs* have enormous degree.
  Generated by **preferential attachment** (new nodes link to already-popular nodes,
  "rich get richer"). Consequence: robust to random failure, fragile to targeted
  hub attack.
- **Weak ties (Granovetter 1973)** — bridges between communities are usually weak
  ties; novel information flows across them, not within tight clusters.

**Centrality — who matters:** degree (how many links), betweenness (how many shortest
paths pass through you), closeness (how near everyone else), and eigenvector
centrality (linked-to by important nodes).

**Link analysis:**
- **PageRank (1998)** — model a *random surfer* who follows links and occasionally
  teleports (damping factor ~0.85); a page's rank is its probability in the walk's
  **stationary distribution**. This is the leading eigenvector of the (damped)
  transition matrix — a [[markov-chains]] on the web graph. Teleportation guarantees
  the chain is ergodic so the stationary distribution exists and is unique.
- **HITS (Kleinberg 1999)** — computes two scores per node: *authorities* (good
  content) and *hubs* (good link lists), each reinforcing the other.

**Community detection** — partition the graph into densely connected groups.
**Modularity** measures how much more within-group edge density exists than chance;
the **Louvain** algorithm greedily maximizes it. Spectral methods cut the graph via
the Laplacian's eigenvectors; see [[spectral-graph-theory-and-clustering]].

**Diffusion & cascades** — how behavior/information spreads: the **independent
cascade** and **linear threshold** models; the **influence maximization** problem
(pick k seeds to maximize spread) is NP-hard but submodular, so greedy gives a
(1−1/e) guarantee. Underlies epidemic modeling (SIR) and viral marketing.

## Complexity & trade-offs
- PageRank is computed by **power iteration** on a sparse matrix — O(edges) per
  iteration, converging in tens of iterations; scales to billions of edges (the
  original web-scale motivation for MapReduce-style computation).
- Exact betweenness is O(V·E) (Brandes' algorithm); expensive on huge graphs, so it
  is sampled.

## Pitfalls & gotchas
- **Fitting a power law by eye** — many "scale-free" claims fail rigorous
  goodness-of-fit; log-log straightness is not proof.
- **Dangling nodes** (no out-links) leak PageRank; handle by redistributing their
  rank uniformly.
- **Confusing correlation with contagion** — homophily (similar people cluster) mimics
  influence in observational social data; you need experiments to separate them.

## Worked example
Ranking a 10-node citation graph: build the column-stochastic transition matrix,
add 0.15 uniform teleportation, and run power iteration `r ← 0.85·M·r + 0.15·(1/n)`
until `r` stops changing. The papers cited by other highly ranked papers accumulate
the most rank — the same computation Google ran on the web.
