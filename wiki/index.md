### §1.2
- [[eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors]] — **Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, diagonalization, and symmetric matrices**: Directions a matrix only stretches (Ax = λx); when n independent ones exist A = XΛX⁻¹ and powers/exponentials become trivial; symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors (A = QΛQᵀ), positive definiteness means all λ > 0, and Markov/PageRank matrices converge to their λ = 1 eigenvector under power iteration. (in: 9)
- [[four-fundamental-subspaces]] — **Four fundamental subspaces, rank, and rank–nullity**: Column space C(A) and nullspace N(A) (with their transposes) organise everything about Ax=b — solvability, uniqueness, dimension counting — via rank r: dim C(A) = dim C(Aᵀ) = r, dim N(A) = n − r, dim N(Aᵀ) = m − r, with row space ⊥ nullspace. (in: 8)
- [[gaussian-elimination-lu]] — **Gaussian elimination, LU factorization, and determinants**: How Ax=b is actually solved — elimination to upper-triangular form, recorded as A = LU (with a permutation for pivoting), back-substitution, ~2n³/3 flops — plus what the pivots tell you (rank, determinant, positive definiteness) and why determinants are for theory, not computation. (in: 9)
- [[k-means-clustering]] — **k-means clustering**: Partition vectors into k groups by minimizing the sum of squared distances to group centroids; Lloyd's alternating assign/recompute algorithm always decreases the objective and converges to a local optimum, so run it several times from k-means++ seeds and pick the best. (in: 2)
- [[least-squares]] — **Least squares, linear regression, and validation**: Minimize ‖Ax − b‖² — solved by the normal equations AᵀAx̂ = Aᵀb, geometrically the projection of b onto the column space, numerically via QR or SVD — and how it becomes linear regression with feature maps, regularization (ridge), and out-of-sample validation to detect overfitting. (in: 16)
- [[matrices-and-linear-maps]] — **Matrices as linear maps (column picture, multiplication, inverses, transformations)**: A matrix is a linear map written in a basis — its columns are where the basis vectors land — so Ax is a combination of columns, AB is composition, the inverse undoes the map, and rotations, projections, selectors, graph incidence matrices and convolutions are all just matrices. (in: 7)
- [[orthogonality-and-projections]] — **Orthogonality, projections, Gram–Schmidt, and QR**: Projecting b onto a subspace gives the closest point; with an orthonormal basis the projection is a sum of inner products, Gram–Schmidt manufactures such a basis (and doubles as an independence test), and A = QR is the numerically sound way to solve least squares. (in: 10)
- [[svd-and-pca]] — **Singular value decomposition (SVD), low-rank approximation, PCA, pseudoinverse**: Every matrix factors as A = UΣVᵀ (orthonormal U, V; nonnegative σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ …), giving rank, condition number, the best rank-k approximation (Eckart–Young), the pseudoinverse for least squares, and PCA when applied to centered data — the workhorse of dimensionality reduction and embeddings. (in: 9)
- [[vectors-and-inner-products]] — **Vectors, inner products, norms, and cosine similarity**: The vector operations behind embeddings and numeric code — inner product, Euclidean and other norms, distance, angle and cosine similarity, standard deviation as a norm, Cauchy–Schwarz and triangle inequalities — with flop counts and the reasons cosine similarity is used for embeddings. (in: 3)

### §1.3
- [[derivatives-and-gradients]] — **Derivatives, chain rule, gradients, Jacobians, Hessians, and Taylor approximation**: The derivative is the best linear approximation; the chain rule composes local linear maps (which is all backpropagation does); gradients point uphill and vanish at optima; the Hessian's definiteness classifies critical points; Taylor's first and second order expansions justify gradient descent and Newton's method. (in: 5)
- [[integrals-and-sums]] — **Integrals, Riemann sums, and bounding sums by integrals**: The integral as a limit of sums and the fundamental theorem that makes it computable; the integral bounds on monotone sums (harmonic numbers, log n!, Σ i^k) that algorithm analysis uses constantly; and the basics of numerical/Monte Carlo integration and continuous expectation. (in: 4)

### §1.4
- [[bayes-theorem-and-inference]] — **Bayes' theorem, base rates, and Bayesian inference**: Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior; why base rates dominate rare-event tests; naive Bayes as Bayes plus a conditional-independence assumption; MAP estimation as regularized MLE; conjugate priors that make updating arithmetic; and the frequentist/Bayesian split on what a probability means. (in: 12)
- [[central-limit-theorem-and-lln]] — **Law of large numbers and the central limit theorem**: Averages of i.i.d. variables converge to the mean (LLN) and their fluctuations are normal with standard deviation σ/√n (CLT) — the basis of standard errors, confidence intervals, Monte Carlo error bars, and the 1/√n cost of precision — with the conditions under which they fail. (in: 7)
- [[common-distributions]] — **Common probability distributions and when they arise**: The named distributions as stories — Bernoulli/binomial (fixed trials), geometric/negative binomial (waiting for successes), Poisson (rare events, binomial limit), uniform, exponential (memoryless waiting), normal (sums), beta (a probability's distribution), gamma — with means, variances, relationships, and how to sample from them. (in: 9)
- [[concentration-inequalities]] — **Concentration inequalities (Markov, Chebyshev, Chernoff, union bound)**: The three tail bounds every randomized-algorithm analysis uses — Markov (mean only), Chebyshev (variance), Chernoff (sum of independent 0/1 variables, exponentially small tails) — plus the union bound, with the rule of thumb for which one is tight enough. (in: 11)
- [[hypothesis-testing-and-confidence-intervals]] — **Hypothesis testing, p-values, confidence intervals, bootstrap, and A/B tests**: How to decide whether an observed effect is real — null hypotheses and p-values, Type I/II errors and power, confidence intervals from the CLT, the bootstrap and permutation tests that need no formulas, A/B testing mechanics, and the ways these are abused (p-hacking, peeking, multiple comparisons). (in: 8)
- [[log-probabilities]] — **Log probabilities and the log-sum-exp trick**: Work with log P instead of P — products become sums, tiny probabilities stop underflowing to zero, and argmax is unchanged — plus the log-sum-exp trick for normalizing (softmax, marginalizing) without overflow. (in: 5)
- [[markov-chains]] — **Markov chains, stationary distributions, and MCMC**: A memoryless process on states with a transition matrix; irreducible aperiodic finite chains converge to a unique stationary distribution π = πP from any start; hitting times come from first-step analysis; and MCMC inverts the picture by designing a chain (Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs) whose stationary distribution is the one you want to sample. (in: 5)
- [[maximum-likelihood-estimation]] — **Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and MAP**: Choose parameters that make the observed data most probable — maximize the log-likelihood of i.i.d. samples — with worked closed forms (Bernoulli, normal, Poisson), the properties that justify it (consistency, asymptotic normality with variance 1/nI(θ)), MAP as MLE plus a log-prior (= regularization), and why minimizing cross-entropy is MLE. (in: 9)
- [[probabilistic-analysis-of-algorithms]] — **Probabilistic analysis of algorithms (expected running time, hashing, quicksort, coupon collector)**: The standard moves for analyzing randomized or average-case algorithms — indicator variables plus linearity of expectation, the law of total expectation on the first step, harmonic sums, balls-in-bins/birthday calculations, coupon collector, and Chernoff bounds for "with high probability" — illustrated on hashing, quicksort, and load balancing. (in: 4)
- [[random-variables-expectation]] — **Random variables, expectation, variance, and conditional expectation**: A random variable is a function on the sample space; PMF/PDF/CDF describe it; expectation is linear regardless of independence (the indicator trick), variance is E[X²] − (E[X])², and conditional expectation with the laws of total expectation and total variance turn messy analyses into first-step recursions. (in: 11)
