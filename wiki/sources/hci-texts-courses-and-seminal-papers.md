# HCI — Texts, Courses, and Seminal Papers (Various)

## What it is
The literature of **human-computer interaction (HCI)**: how to design, prototype,
and evaluate interactive systems so that people can use them effectively. HCI sits
at the intersection of computer science, cognitive psychology, and design. It is
taught as studio courses (build and test a real interface) far more than as lecture
courses, because the craft is in the iteration.

## Key ideas
- **The design of everyday things (Norman)** — affordances, signifiers, mapping,
  feedback, constraints, and the *gulfs of execution and evaluation*. See
  [[human-computer-interaction]] and [[interaction-design-and-cognitive-models]].
- **Usability engineering (Nielsen)** — the 10 heuristics, discount usability,
  and "five users find most problems." See [[usability-evaluation-and-user-research]].
- **User-centered / iterative design** — prototype → test with users → revise,
  cheaply and often. See [[usability-evaluation-and-user-research]].
- **Human information processing** — the *Model Human Processor* and *GOMS*
  (Card, Moran & Newell 1983); **Fitts's law** for pointing time. See
  [[interaction-design-and-cognitive-models]].

## Chapter / lecture map
- **Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things* (1988/2013)** — the foundational text
  on cognitive design principles; source of "Norman doors."
- **Krug, *Don't Make Me Think* (2000)** — web usability, self-evident design.
- **Sharp, Preece & Rogers, *Interaction Design* (Rogers et al.)** — the standard
  survey textbook: process, cognition, evaluation.
- **Cooper, *About Face* / *The Inmates Are Running the Asylum*** — goal-directed
  design, personas, interaction design as a discipline.
- **Nielsen, *Usability Engineering* (1993)** — heuristics and the discount methods.
- **Lazar, Feng & Hochheiser, *Research Methods in HCI*** — how to run studies.
- **Stanford CS147 (Intro to HCI Design)** and **CS247 (studio)** — needfinding,
  paper prototyping, heuristic evaluation, and usability testing in a project loop.
- **Maryland / Shneiderman & Plaisant, *Designing the User Interface*** — the
  Eight Golden Rules; direct manipulation.

## Notable claims & quotes
- Norman: "**Good design is actually a lot harder to notice than poor design**,
  in part because good designs fit our needs so well that the design is invisible."
- Nielsen: "**A wide range of usability problems can be found with only five users.**"
- Buxton: "You can't do sketching-level exploration with high-fidelity tools."

## Founding papers (the HCI spine)
- **Vannevar Bush, "As We May Think" (1945)** — the *memex*: an associative,
  linked personal knowledge machine; the intellectual ancestor of hypertext and
  the Web. See [[human-computer-interaction]].
- **Douglas Engelbart, "Augmenting Human Intellect" (1962)** and the 1968
  **"Mother of All Demos"** — the mouse, hypertext, windows, real-time
  collaboration, video conferencing, all shown at once.
- **Ivan Sutherland, *Sketchpad* (1963)** — the first interactive graphical
  interface; direct manipulation and constraints; ancestor of GUIs and CAD.
- **Card, Moran & Newell, *The Psychology of Human-Computer Interaction* (1983)**
  — the Model Human Processor and GOMS; HCI as a quantitative science.
- **Fitts (1954)** — the speed-accuracy law of rapid aimed movement, now the
  most-used quantitative model in interface design.

## What it adds
Where the rest of the wiki treats the machine, HCI treats the *human in the loop*:
the interface is where correctness meets cognition. It connects to
[[web-development-and-cross-platform]] (front-end is applied HCI),
[[data-visualization]] (visual encoding is perceptual HCI), and
[[computing-ethics-and-professional-responsibility]] (dark patterns, consent, and
accessibility are HCI ethics).
